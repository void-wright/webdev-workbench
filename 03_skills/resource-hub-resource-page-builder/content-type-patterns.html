Resource Hub: Content Type Implementation Patterns

A page-layout reference for every content type in an open resource hub. Use this when building your hub: each content type has a specific structure, required elements, and CTA placement rules. The patterns make useful content easy to read and share while keeping a visible Side Door for buyers ready to talk.

Companion to 03_skills/resource-hub-resource-page-builder/SKILL.md. Print-friendly. Brand-neutral; apply your site's visual system on top.

Legend

  • Required always present in this pattern
  • Optional include if you have it
  • Side Door CTA buyer enquiry; persistent or contextual
  • Never blocks access or undermines trust

The framework in 60 seconds

Readers arrive with different intentions. Some want to learn; others are ready to buy. A hub built entirely around gated downloads and sales forms can collect contact details while driving away readers who only need an answer. Design for learning and sharing, with a clear route to purchase or contact alongside the content.

Three complementary layers:

  • Front Door (Demand Creation): open content people can share in planning meetings, community groups, social posts, email, and everyday conversations.
  • Side Door (Demand Capture): visible CTAs such as "Ask about equipment," "Find a stockist," or "Request a quote," never blocking content.
  • Trust Infrastructure: sources, customer voices, clear pricing, and self-reported attribution.

Two rules guide forms in these patterns: use a free-text "How did you hear about us?" field rather than a dropdown, and place no content gates on Front Door educational content.

Universal

Universal page structure

Every page on the hub, regardless of content type, shares this skeleton. Patterns 1–9 modify the body region and its supporting details.

Persistent Side Door CTA · in site header [Logo] [Nav]    [Ask about equipment] button
Title block Page title · byline · date
Above-fold summary 2–3 sentences: what this is, why it matters
Body Varies by content type → Patterns 1–9
Contextual Side Door CTA · end of body "Planning your kitchen layout? [Ask about equipment]"
Related content 3–4 cards keeping reader on hub
Footer Site nav · legal · optional signup with attribution
Anti-pattern Popup that blocks content / email wall

Required on every page

  • AlwaysPersistent header CTA visible on scroll. Keep it available on narrow screens too.
  • AlwaysTitle, byline, date. Readers want to know who said this and when.
  • AlwaysAbove-fold summary in 2–3 sentences. Reuse its key message in social preview metadata and search descriptions.
  • AlwaysFree-text "How did you hear about us?" on every form, including newsletter, contact, quote, and booking forms. Not a dropdown.
  • AlwaysEnd-of-body contextual CTA using the same Side Door target with page-aware copy, except where a pattern makes it optional.

Never

  • NeverPopup that interrupts reading.
  • NeverEmail wall / content gate on Front Door content.
  • NeverDropdown for "How did you hear about us?" It limits answers to categories you already expected.
  • NeverHidden header CTA. Buyers who are ready to act need to find the next step immediately.
Why this matters Help readers who are not yet buying: they can learn, share, and remember you later. Keep a clear path for those ready to buy now. A page that demands an enquiry before offering value loses opportunities to earn trust.
Pattern 1

Web-native long-form essay / cornerstone

The page is the asset. No PDF, no embed, no download required. Use it for category explainers, practical viewpoints, and founder essays. A working range is 1,500–4,000 words, with length determined by the topic. A useful essay gives readers a link worth sharing.

Persistent CTA "Find a stockist" in header
Title (H1) Prominent editorial title · 32–48px
Byline · date · read-time By [Author] · [Publication date] · [Estimated reading time]
Above-fold summary 2–3 sentence dek
Body · prose H2 / H3 structure, pull quotes, occasional figures, citations
Inline figures Charts, diagrams, photos with captions
Citations / references Numbered list at the end of body
Social share buttons LinkedIn · X · Copy link
Contextual CTA · end of body Topic-aware Side Door link
Related essays 3–4 cards

What it is

The cornerstone of the Front Door. A web-native essay is designed to be read on the page, shared as a link, and referenced in buying guides or project plans. No download is required because the page is the artifact.

Required elements

  • Title in a readable headline font suited to your site, giving this pattern a clear editorial identity
  • Author byline with relevant experience so readers can assess the voice
  • Date, because advice can become outdated as products and practices change
  • 2–3 sentence dek above the fold, with matching social preview metadata
  • H2 / H3 body structure for scan-readers
  • Citations as numbered references at the bottom of the body, supporting factual claims
  • Social share buttons such as LinkedIn, X, and Copy link to make sharing easy

Form placement

  • Side Door CTA persistent in header
  • Side Door CTA end-of-body, contextual ("Choosing parts for your next build? [Find a stockist]")
  • Optional sidebar "Subscribe to our guides" with an email field and free-text attribution

Open-access rules

  • Never Gate this content with an email form to read.
  • Never Truncate it with "click to read more"; keep the full essay on one page.
  • Always Include a printable / readable view with no popup interruptions.

AEO / SEO checklist

  • Schema.org Article or BlogPosting
  • Title tag around 50–60 chars; target topic + brand
  • Meta description around 140–160 chars summarizing the dek
  • Author block with bio + relevant experience to support reader trust
  • 3+ useful internal links to related hub pages
  • State the thesis in the opening paragraph so readers and automated systems can identify the main point
Illustrative examples An invented bicycle-parts company, Alderwheel Components, could publish an essay on choosing repairable parts for everyday commuting. An invented kitchen-equipment maker, Kettlebridge Works, could explain how kitchen layout affects access for cleaning. Both teach a decision rather than recite a product catalogue.
Pattern 2

Blog post

Shorter, more topical version of Pattern 1. A working range is 500–1,500 words. Publish on a sustainable cadence, such as weekly or biweekly. Include an author headshot and tags, and address timely questions in your field.

Persistent CTA "Ask about supplies" / "Visit the shop" in header
Title (H1) Specific, topical, often question-form
Author block Headshot · name · role · date · tags
Above-fold summary 2–3 concise sentences
Body 500–1,500 words, conversational, links out generously
Social share buttons LinkedIn · X · Copy link
Contextual CTA End of body, topic-aware
Related posts 3 cards by tag

What it is

Regular content that is shorter, more conversational, and responsive to current questions. Where Pattern 1 develops a broad argument, Pattern 2 covers a focused topic with a point of view. An invented garden-supply shop, Fernbasket Supply, might explain how to choose containers for a small patio.

Required elements

  • Author headshot + role to identify the person behind the advice
  • Tags / categories to drive the related-posts feed
  • Conversational tone and useful links to other sources; a blog is not an island

Form placement

  • Side Door CTA persistent in header
  • Side Door CTA end-of-body
  • Optional "Subscribe to the blog" CTA in footer / sidebar, with email and free-text attribution fields

Open-access rules

  • Never Gate the post.
  • Never Use "Read more" pagination to drive page views.
  • Always Allow direct links to subheadings using HTML IDs so readers can share specific sections.

AEO / SEO checklist

  • Schema.org BlogPosting
  • Title tag around 50–60 chars; use a question when it matches the reader's intent
  • Meta description around 140–160 chars, describing the answer the post provides
  • Author with bio + relevant experience
  • Useful tag and category pages discoverable; canonical URL set
  • Answer the implied question in the first paragraph
Pattern 3

PDF asset with landing page (brochure / guide / datasheet / spec sheet)

If you have a PDF, create a landing page for it. The PDF is supporting; the landing page is primary. The landing page provides search context and a useful link for private sharing; the PDF serves offline reading and printing.

Persistent CTA "Discuss a commission" in header
Title + subtitle Reflects the PDF's title
Above-fold summary · 2 paragraphs Plain-language: what's inside, who it's for, what they'll learn
"What's inside" bullets · 5–7 The TOC reframed as benefits
Embedded PDF viewer PDF.js or native browser PDF embed
Download button [↓ Download PDF] · NO email gate
Never Email gate on download
Contextual CTA After the embed
Related content Other resources on the same topic

What it is

You have a PDF brochure, guide, datasheet, or spec sheet. Give it a real page that introduces the asset, summarizes it, embeds it for inline reading, and offers an ungated download for offline use. An invented ceramic studio, Pebbleturn Studio, could use this pattern for its tableware dimensions and care guide.

Why a landing page (not just a PDF link)

  • SEO: PDFs can be indexed, but an HTML page gives you more control over navigation, structured content, and the reader's next step.
  • Private sharing: a useful summary and preview metadata give context when the URL appears in social messages or email.
  • Accessibility: accessible HTML gives essential information to readers on phones, screen readers, or devices that cannot open the PDF.
  • Analytics: you can measure page visits and download actions separately, subject to your site's consent settings.

Required elements

  • Title and subtitle that match the PDF
  • 2-paragraph above-fold summary in plain language, rather than a pasted introduction
  • 5–7 "What's inside" bullets, restating the contents as reader benefits where the asset supports that detail
  • Embedded PDF viewer such as PDF.js or a native iframe, with a direct link as a fallback
  • Download button, clearly labeled and ungated

Form placement

  • Side Door CTA persistent header
  • Side Door CTA end of page, contextual
  • Never A form between the user and the download button

Open-access rules

  • Never Gate a public educational download just to collect email addresses. Keep account-specific documents, private order details, and event registration separate from the open resource library.
  • Always If the PDF works as web content, rewrite it as a Pattern 1 web-native essay and retire it as the primary reading format. Retain a printable companion where useful.
  • Always Add file size and page count next to the download button to set expectations.

AEO / SEO checklist

  • Schema.org Article when the landing page is an article; do not describe the entire page as only a PDF MediaObject
  • Title tag matches the asset title
  • Meta description around 140–160 chars summarizing what's inside
  • "What's inside" bullets phrased as complete natural-language statements
  • Canonical URL on the landing page, with consistent canonical signals for equivalent PDF content where supported
  • Make the summary and bullets above the embed complete enough to help readers without opening the file
Anti-pattern · the email gate "Enter your email to download." This adds friction to an otherwise useful public resource. A submitted address may mean only "I need the guide," not "I want to buy." Keep the download open and offer a separate, relevant enquiry link.
How to retire a PDF Repeated requests for a PDF suggest that its content deserves a discoverable web page. Turn it into a Pattern 1 essay or guide, then keep the PDF as a printable companion if readers still need it.
Pattern 4

Someone else wrote about you

A local paper, a blog or a trade newsletter mentions your business. The article stays on their site. Your hub gets a short page (or just a card) that says in your own words what it covers and sends readers to the original.

Persistent CTA "Plan a bakery order" in header
Title The article's own title, as a link to it
Where it appeared · 1 line "Featured in the Riverside Weekly, spring edition"
Your summary · 2–4 sentences What the piece is about, in your words
Link out [↗ Read it where it was published]
Never A copy of the article or its PDF on your hub
Related pages 2–3 of your own guides on the same topic

What it is

A pointer to something another person published about you. For example, an invented regional bakery chain, Coppercrumb Bakery, might add a short page about a local paper's piece on its early-morning baking shift. The page exists so visitors who hear about the article can find it, and so the hub links to it from a place you control.

Why NOT host a copy

  • Rights. Being allowed to link to an article does not mean you may republish it.
  • Accuracy. The writer keeps the original up to date, including any corrections.
  • Trust. Readers can see the original words and judge them for themselves.

Required elements

  • The article's title, linked to the original
  • One plain line saying where it appeared
  • A short summary in your own words; quote a line only if the source allows it, and mark it as a quote
  • A clear link out to the original

Form placement

  • Side Door CTA persistent header
  • Optional contextual CTA at end of page ("Planning refreshments for a local event? [Plan a bakery order]")
  • No form on the page itself.

Open-access rules

  • Never Copy the article's text or file onto your hub.
  • Never Put your summary behind an email wall.
  • Always Keep the writer's words and your own words clearly apart.

AEO / SEO checklist

  • The link to the original is a normal, visible text link
  • Your summary is in the page text, not in an image
  • The page's usual markup is enough; if you use Article for your summary, you are its author
  • Link to your own related guides so the page is not a dead end
When you have several Put them on one "Written about us" page, newest first, one card each. Each card links straight to the original. A short list is fine; it does not need filters.
Pattern 5

Video / webinar / conference talk recording

Embed the player from the platform; the hub is the home, and YouTube/Vimeo/Wistia supplies the player. Include a reviewed transcript so the content is readable, accessible, and available as page text for search.

Persistent CTA "Ask about equipment" in header
Title · speaker · duration "Planning a Compact Prep Area · [Product designer] · [Duration]"
Embedded video player YouTube / Vimeo / Wistia iframe (16:9)
Summary · 2–3 sentences What the video covers
Key takeaways · bullets · 4–6 Skimmable for scan-readers
Full transcript · collapsible Speaker-attributed; search context + accessibility
Speaker bio(s) Photo · title · org · short bio
Slide deck download [↓ Slides PDF] · NO email gate
Contextual CTA After takeaways
Related videos 3 cards

What it is

Any video asset: business explainer, product demo, webinar replay, conference talk recording, or customer interview. Use the same pattern for each: embed the player and surround it with text that makes the content discoverable and scannable.

Why embed (not link to YouTube)

  • Keeps the viewer on your hub so they can navigate to other content after watching.
  • Gives the topic a dedicated page that can be discovered independently of the video platform.
  • Places the recording beside its summary, sources, transcript, and related resources.

Required elements

  • Title area that names the speaker and duration so people know what they're committing to
  • Responsive embedded player at 16:9 ratio when that matches the source video
  • 2–3 sentence summary for readers who want the gist before watching
  • 4–6 bulleted key takeaways
  • Full transcript, speaker-attributed if multiple speakers and collapsible if very long; provide captions in the player as well

Webinar-specific additions

  • Presenter bio block with headshot
  • Slide deck download as an ungated PDF
  • Q&A excerpts presented as useful standalone answers
  • Link to register for upcoming webinars

Conference talk-specific additions

  • Conference name + date + location ("Presented at [Event], [City], [Date]")
  • Link to conference site
  • Attribution to the organizer if applicable

Form placement

  • Side Door CTA persistent header
  • Side Door CTA after the takeaways block ("Planning a similar kitchen setup? [Ask about equipment]")
  • Optional "View upcoming webinars" CTA in sidebar

Open-access rules

  • Never Skip the transcript. It provides a text alternative and makes the content easier to search and quote.
  • Never Gate the recording. Webinar replays are Front Door content.
  • Never Auto-play the video on page load. It interrupts readers and can add unnecessary loading work.
  • Always When automatic captions or transcription are available, use them as a starting point and correct names, terminology, and speaker labels.
  • Always If the video is on your YouTube channel, ensure the channel description links back to your hub.

AEO / SEO checklist

  • Schema.org VideoObject with accurate transcript, thumbnailUrl, uploadDate, and duration; use contentUrl for the media file or embedUrl for a player as appropriate
  • Transcript ON THE PAGE; captions inside an external player do not provide equivalent text in your page's HTML
  • Chapter markers / timestamps with Clip schema where applicable
  • Named speakers with relevant experience identified accurately
  • Title tag: speaker + topic
  • Chapters provide direct references to specific moments in the video
The transcript is the asset Some visitors will read the page without watching the recording. Give them a complete summary and a well-edited transcript. Use the recording as a source for a readable page with the same care you would give a Pattern 1 essay.
Pattern 6

Podcast episode

Embed the audio player. Add a transcript, episode notes, guest bio, and listen-on links to the platforms carrying the episode. Use the same logic as video: the page is the home, and the platform supplies the player.

Persistent CTA "Find a stockist" in header
Episode title · number · date "Ep. [Number] · Choosing Parts for Everyday Riding · with [Guest]"
Audio player Spotify / Apple / native HTML5 embed
Listen-on links Available listening platforms · RSS
Episode notes · 2–3 paragraphs What the episode covers + key quotes
Full transcript Speaker-attributed
Key timestamps · chapters "[Timestamp]: How do you check parts compatibility?"
Guest + host bios Photo · org · related episodes
Contextual CTA After the notes

What it is

An episode of your own podcast or one you appeared on. Either way, the hub gets a page so you can provide context and a stable reference. Alderwheel Components, the invented bicycle-parts company, could host notes for a conversation with a bicycle shop mechanic.

Required elements

  • Embedded audio player using the host's supported embed or native HTML audio when you have the file and permission
  • Listen-on links to the platforms that carry the episode, including RSS where available
  • Episode notes: 2–3 paragraphs of what's covered, with 1–2 accurate pull-quotes from the conversation
  • Full transcript with speaker attribution

Form placement

  • Side Door CTA persistent header
  • Side Door CTA after episode notes, contextual
  • Optional "Subscribe to the podcast" CTA with direct subscribe links per platform

Open-access rules

  • Never Skip the transcript. It provides the same accessibility and text-discovery benefits as the video transcript.
  • Always Provide chapters / timestamps for episodes longer than 20 minutes so listeners can find relevant sections.
  • Always Prepare a short audiogram, such as a 30–60 second captioned clip, for promotion on an appropriate social channel.

AEO / SEO checklist

  • Schema.org PodcastEpisode with partOfSeries linking to the show
  • Full transcript on page
  • Episode number + season visible where the show uses them
  • Guest identity and relevant experience represented accurately
  • Chapters / timestamps
  • Transcripts and chapters make individual answers easier to locate and reference
Pattern 7

Customer case study / outcome story

Web-native page. Named customer and organization, with permission. Measured results and the customer's own quote. A strong case study shows what happened and under what conditions; generic praise gives readers little to assess.

Persistent CTA "Ask about supplies" in header
Customer block Photo · name · title · organization
Headline · outcome-focused "How [Community Garden] Simplified Its Seasonal Tool Setup"
Problem framing What they were trying to improve, in their words
Approach What they did, with practical specifics
Quantified results Setup time · replacement costs · comparison period
Customer quote · pulled out Named, in their voice, ideally specific
"What it means" · 1 paragraph Implications for similar buyers
Contextual CTA "Equipping a shared garden? [Ask about supplies]"
Never "Our customers love us!" generic testimonials

What it is

A real customer telling a specific story with verifiable results. Explain who they are, what they wanted to improve, what they did, what changed, and what the experience means for buyers in similar situations. Fernbasket Supply, the invented garden shop, could use this structure for a community garden customer story.

Required elements

  • Named customer with photo, title, and organization, where permission allows; identification helps readers assess the account
  • Outcome-focused headline describing what the customer accomplished
  • Problem framing in the customer's words, gathered through an interview
  • Quantified results such as time saved, costs changed, or repeat work avoided, with a baseline and comparison period
  • Pull-quote from the customer that is attributed and specific
  • "What it means for similar buyers" with the conditions and limitations preserved
  • Explicit permission from the customer and anyone authorized to approve publication for their organization

Form placement

  • Side Door CTA persistent header
  • Side Door CTA end-of-page, highly contextual ("Planning shared storage for garden tools? [Ask about supplies]")

Open-access rules

  • Never Put vendor-written praise in a customer's mouth. Readers can question the whole story when a quote sounds like a sales script.
  • Never Anonymize the customer without explaining why. If privacy requires it, label the story clearly, such as "Customer name withheld at their request."
  • Always Show verified measurements where results are claimed. If they are unavailable, state that limitation rather than inventing precision.
  • Always Use a real, approved quote from a customer interview. Preserve the customer's wording and meaning.

AEO / SEO checklist

  • Schema.org Article with mentions of the customer organization where public
  • Quantified results in plain text on the page, not only inside images, so they remain accessible and readily extractable
  • Approved customer identities surfaced accurately in Person and Organization structured data
  • Internal links to related case studies
  • State who changed what, over which period, and how the result was measured; keep qualifications beside the number
Anti-pattern · the testimonial wall A grid of customer logos with one-line praise can provide a quick introduction to customer relationships. It does not replace a case study that explains the problem, approach, and result. Use each for its own purpose.
Co-author when possible Draft with the customer, invite their edits, and credit contributions accurately. Their review helps preserve the facts and voice. A story they recognize as their own is also easier for them to share with their network.
Pattern 8

Practical guide / methodology / how-to page

Web-native and step-based. Include materials, steps, considerations, and troubleshooting. Offer an optional downloadable PDF for use away from a screen. For a ceramic studio, this can also explain how to plan a commission or what to expect from a workshop.

Persistent CTA "Ask the studio" in header
Title · last updated date "How to Plan a Custom Tableware Set · Updated [Date]"
Overview + expected outcomes "What you'll have ready after following this guide"
Materials list Required + recommended
Step-by-step · numbered Each step: action · why it matters · gotcha
Considerations / troubleshooting Edge cases, common errors, how to fix
Downloadable PDF Print-friendly format · ungated
Contextual CTA "Need help choosing dimensions? [Ask the studio]"
Related guides 3 cards

What it is

A step-by-step guide that helps the reader complete a task. Pebbleturn Studio, the invented ceramic studio, could explain how to measure storage space, decide on serving pieces, and prepare a commission brief. Keep the guide practical, sequential, and easy to troubleshoot.

Required elements

  • Prominent last-updated date so readers can assess whether the instructions are current
  • Expected outcome / "what you'll get" framing up front
  • Materials / requirements list before the steps
  • Numbered steps with one action per step
  • Per-step gotchas and explanations of why the action matters
  • Troubleshooting section at the end

Form placement

  • Side Door CTA persistent header; use help-oriented wording such as "Ask the studio" when the reader is mid-task
  • Side Door CTA end of page, problem-aware ("Unsure whether your measurements are complete? [Ask the studio]")

Open-access rules

  • Never Gate the guide. Readers need immediate access while doing the task.
  • Always Provide a print-friendly view with clear step numbers and no unnecessary site navigation.
  • Always Date-stamp every guide with the last review date. Outdated instructions can cause wasted effort or incorrect results.
  • Always Include a "what success looks like" image or output where relevant.

AEO / SEO checklist

  • Schema.org HowTo with accurate step, tool, supply, and totalTime where applicable
  • Numbered step-by-step format that is readable and machine-parseable
  • Materials list as structured HowToSupply where applicable
  • Time estimate visible and consistent with totalTime
  • Structured procedures help describe the task; markup alone does not guarantee a search feature or ranking
Why how-to guides support discovery People search for specific tasks and troubleshooting help. A clear page that answers those questions can introduce the business while helping someone finish useful work. Review it regularly so the advice remains dependable.
Pattern 9

Topic / category / SEO-anchored page

Essay-format page anchored to a search-relevant topic. A regional bakery chain might explain "How to Choose Bread for a Shared Lunch"; a garden shop might explain container materials. Build the page to answer a real question for readers who do not yet know your brand.

Persistent CTA "Plan a bakery order" in header
Title (H1) · target keyword Topic name in plain language
Schema.org structured data Article / CollectionPage / other matching type
Above-fold summary 2–3 sentences answering the implicit search query
Body · H2 sections "What it is" · "Available options" · "Tradeoffs" · "Common questions"
Citations · numbered Reliable sources supporting factual claims
Cross-links to related topics "See also: [bread storage], [serving ideas]"
Contextual CTA Topic-specific ("Organizing a shared lunch? [Plan a bakery order]")
Related topics hub Cards by topic family

What it is

A page built to serve readers searching for a topic rather than your brand. Product-category explainers, selection guides, and care overviews can each answer distinct questions. For Coppercrumb Bakery, the invented regional chain, a bread-selection guide could connect readers to relevant serving and storage articles.

Why this pattern is leveraged

  • Each useful topic page provides another potential entry point from organic search
  • Relevant cross-links help readers and crawlers discover connected information
  • Topic pages also teach categories and choices before a reader is ready to buy

Required elements

  • Target topic expressed naturally in H1, URL slug, meta description, and the first 100 words
  • Schema.org structured data matching the actual page, such as Article for an explainer
  • Body organized around real reader questions such as "What is it?" "How do the options differ?" and "Which details affect my choice?"
  • Citations to reliable sources so readers can verify factual claims
  • Cross-links to 3–5 related topic pages where useful

Form placement

  • Side Door CTA persistent header
  • Side Door CTA end of body, highly topic-specific

Open-access rules

  • Never Stuff keywords. Repetition that makes the writing awkward does not help the reader.
  • Never Turn the page into a product pitch. Teach the topic and mention products only where they help explain a choice.
  • Always Cite sources for factual claims and distinguish evidence from opinion.
  • Always Review the page when products, source material, or practical advice changes.

AEO / SEO checklist

  • Schema.org Article, CollectionPage, or another type that accurately describes the page
  • Target topic in H1, URL slug, meta description, and first 100 words of body
  • FAQ block with FAQPage structured data if it matches the visible content; do not assume rich-result eligibility
  • 5+ useful internal links when the topic and available library justify them
  • Citations to reliable primary sources where available
  • Answer the implied query directly in the opening; structure as Q&A where natural
Build at scale For a bakery resource library, create distinct pages about choosing, serving, storing, and ordering baked goods. Use a consistent structure and connect related topics. Expand only when each page answers a separate reader need and can be kept accurate.
Special case

Press releases: they live in the newsroom, not the hub

Press releases are business announcements. The resource hub contains material people choose to read for practical help. Give those different audiences and tasks distinct places on the site.

Newsroom location A dedicated news or press section on your domain
Newsroom page List of press releases · reverse chronological
Per-release page Headline · date · body · contact · boilerplate
Cross-link to hub Releases that announce content link to the hub asset
Never Press releases inside the hub

Why separate?

  • Different audiences. Journalists look for announcements; customers and prospective buyers look for guidance.
  • Different jobs. Press releases announce what's new; the hub explains topics and helps people make decisions.
  • Different search intent. Company announcements answer different queries from practical guides. Separate navigation makes both easier to find.

How they connect

  • A press release about an article someone wrote about you → links to your short page that points to it (Pattern 4)
  • A press release announcing a new product → links to the product page and any relevant hub guide
  • A press release announcing an expansion → can link to a founder essay on the hub when it adds useful context (Pattern 1)

Open-access rules

  • Never Label a press release as a guide or case study to place it in the hub. The label should match what the reader receives.
  • Always Keep newsroom URLs stable so old releases remain available to people who reference them.

Form placement reference (across all patterns)

Side Door CTAs and forms appear in specific places without blocking content. The persistent header CTA provides a dependable route to an enquiry; other prompts respond to the page topic. Include free-text "How did you hear about us?" on each form.

Universal open-access rules across all patterns

  • Never Gate Front Door content with an email wall.
  • Never Use a popup that blocks content.
  • Never Use a dropdown for "How did you hear about us?" It restricts the answers you can learn from.
  • Never Auto-play video on page load.
  • Never Republish someone else's PDF without permission or an appropriate license.
  • Never Hide pricing on a page about cost; explain variable pricing and any limits on the information available.
  • Always Show the persistent Side Door CTA in the header.
  • Always Include free-text attribution on every form.
  • Always Include a transcript for any video / audio asset.
  • Always Include a 2–3 sentence summary above the fold, expanding it where a pattern calls for more context.
  • Always Cite reliable sources for factual claims.
  • Always Date-stamp content and show the last review date for how-to guides and topic pages.

Universal AEO / SEO rules across all patterns

Open, evidence-rich content gives readers and automated discovery systems useful material to inspect. It does not guarantee rankings or citations. The rules below add the technical mechanics that make your content accessible, understandable, and easier to verify. Per-pattern checklists appear above; this section covers every page.

Technical foundation (every page)

  • HTTPS; good Core Web Vitals targets (LCP < 2.5s, CLS < 0.1, INP < 200ms)
  • Mobile-first responsive design
  • A discoverable XML sitemap and appropriate crawler directives at the site root
  • Canonical URL on every page to identify the preferred version of duplicate content
  • Title tags around 50–60 chars; meta descriptions around 140–160 chars, with clarity taking priority over exact length
  • Open Graph + Twitter Card metadata so social previews have suitable titles, descriptions, and images
  • Author E-E-A-T signals: real authors with bios, headshots, relevant experience, and links to their work. No fake author personas.
  • Published date AND last-reviewed date visible on every content page
  • 3+ useful, semantically related internal links per page where the library supports them

AEO-specific (LLM optimization: Google AI Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Claude)

  • Answer the question / state the thesis in the opening paragraph so the main point is easy to identify
  • Use clear question-answer format where natural
  • Cite authoritative sources so factual claims can be checked
  • Quantify claims when you have reliable measurements, including context and limitations
  • Replace vague praise with concrete features, tradeoffs, and evidence
  • Date-stamp content visibly and review it when the underlying information changes
  • Author identity must be real and verifiable
  • Use accurate structured data matching the visible content to describe entities and relationships
  • Surface quantified results in plain text on the page, not only in images or PDFs, so they remain accessible and easy to extract

Never (open access + AEO converge)

  • Never Keyword stuff at the expense of readable answers
  • Never Create doorway pages that repeat thin content merely to target similar queries
  • Never Publish AI-generated thin content without knowledgeable review
  • Never Invent author personas presented as real people
  • Never Cloak content by showing crawlers materially different information from users
  • Never Hide available pricing on a page whose purpose is to explain cost
  • Never Bury key measurements inside PDFs only; state them with their context on the page
Why open access and discoverability work together Open pages are easier to share and available to permitted crawlers without a form submission. Clear sources help readers verify claims. Plain-text answers, meaningful headings, and accurate metadata make the same information easier for software to interpret. These practices serve people first while giving discovery systems accessible material to work with.
